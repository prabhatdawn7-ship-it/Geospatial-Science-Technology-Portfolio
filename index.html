GIS background

A Geographic Information System (GIS) is a framework for capturing, storing, analysing and presenting data linked to locations on the Earth's surface. It brings together hardware, software, data, methods and people to answer spatial questions: where things are, how they are arranged, and how they change over time.

GIS works by stacking thematic layers of the same place – roads, rivers, land cover, elevation, administrative boundaries – so that relationships between them can be measured and mapped. This layered view is what connects all of my work, from field surveys at Pashan Lake to deep-learning road extraction in the Western Ghats.

Core concepts I work with

Spatial data models

Vector data stores discrete features as points (sample sites, trees), lines (roads, streams) and polygons (lakes, talukas). Raster data stores continuous surfaces as grids of cells, such as satellite images, DEMs and NDVI maps.

Coordinate reference systems

Every layer needs a CRS. Geographic systems such as WGS 84 (EPSG:4326) use degrees; projected systems such as UTM Zone 43N (EPSG:32643), which covers Pune and Satara, use metres and are needed for accurate distance, area and length.

Remote sensing

Satellites record reflected and emitted energy in spectral bands. Sensors like Landsat, Sentinel-2, MODIS and LISS-IV differ in spatial, spectral and temporal resolution, which decides what can be mapped – LISS-IV's 5.8 m pixels can resolve narrow rural roads.

Spatial analysis

Buffers, overlays, zonal statistics, terrain analysis (slope, drainage) and spectral indices such as NDVI for vegetation health and NDWI for water turn raw layers into measurable environmental indicators.

Spatial databases and WebGIS

PostgreSQL/PostGIS stores geometry with spatial indexes and SQL functions. GeoServer publishes those tables as OGC web services (WMS, WFS), which feed interactive dashboards built with QGIS, ArcGIS and JavaScript.

GeoAI

GeoAI applies machine learning and deep learning to geospatial data. Semantic segmentation networks – CNNs, Transformers and hybrids such as TransUNet and DPMSRE-Net – label every pixel, enabling automated extraction of features like rural roads at landscape scale.

GIS background and study area

A GIS represents the world as vector features – points, lines and polygons – and raster grids, each linked to attributes. As data grows, a spatial database becomes the single source of truth. PostGIS adds geometry types, a GiST spatial index and hundreds of ST_ functions to PostgreSQL, so every analysis step is a reproducible SQL query.

Online data usually arrives in WGS 84 (EPSG:4326), measured in degrees. Pune lies at about 73.8°E in UTM Zone 43N (EPSG:32643), so all layers are stored in metres for distance and area calculations and transformed back to EPSG:4326 for the web. GeoServer publishes the PostGIS tables as OGC web services – WMS for rendered maps and WFS for the features themselves – which QGIS, ArcGIS and browsers can all read.

Study area. Pune sits on the Deccan Plateau at the confluence of the Mula and Mutha rivers, which flow on as the Mula-Mutha into the Bhima. The city draws its water from the Khadakwasla reservoir system (Khadakwasla, Panshet, Varasgaon, Temghar). Rapid urban growth has sealed land and encroached on streams, monsoon rain repeatedly floods low-lying areas – as in the September 2019 Ambil Odha flash flood – while peripheral areas face seasonal water stress.

Summary: GIS and PostGIS provide the foundation for this project: all data is stored in one spatial database in a metric CRS (EPSG:32643) and shared through GeoServer. Pune was chosen because it combines river flooding, rapid urban growth and seasonal water stress in one city.

Risk model: WSERI

The analysis uses a GIS-based multi-criteria approach. For each ward, four indicators are computed in PostGIS, scaled from 0 to 1 (higher means more risk) and combined into the Water Security & Environmental Risk Index.

CodeIndicatorPostGIS derivationWeight
I1Flood exposureShare of ward inside 200 m river buffers0.35
I2Surface-water scarcity1 − share of ward covered by lakes and reservoirs0.25
I3Imperviousness proxyRoad density, km per km²0.25
I4Water-body remotenessDistance to nearest water body0.15

WSERI = 0.35·I1 + 0.25·I2 + 0.25·I3 + 0.15·I4

CREATE EXTENSION IF NOT EXISTS postgis;

-- load with ogr2ogr, re-projecting to UTM 43N:
-- ogr2ogr -f PostgreSQL PG:"dbname=pune_gis user=postgres" gis_osm_roads_free_1.shp \

CREATE TABLE pune_roads AS
SELECT r.osm_id, r.name, r.fclass,
       ST_Multi(ST_CollectionExtract(ST_Intersection(r.geom, b.geom), 2))::geometry(MultiLineString,32643) AS geom
FROM roads_raw r JOIN pune_boundary b ON ST_Intersects(r.geom, b.geom);

CREATE INDEX ON pune_roads USING GIST (geom);
SELECT f_table_name, type, srid FROM geometry_columns;

SQL: create the 18 features 
CREATE TABLE sites_pt (id serial PRIMARY KEY, site_name text, source_osm_id text,
  geom geometry(Point,32643));
INSERT INTO sites_pt (site_name, source_osm_id, geom)
SELECT COALESCE(name,'Unnamed water body') || ' - monitoring site', osm_id, ST_PointOnSurface(geom)
FROM pune_waterbodies ORDER BY ST_Area(geom) DESC LIMIT 6;

CREATE TABLE access_ln (id serial PRIMARY KEY, site_id int REFERENCES sites_pt(id),
  road_name text, length_m numeric, geom geometry(LineString,32643));
INSERT INTO access_ln (site_id, road_name, length_m, geom)
SELECT s.id, r.name, ROUND(ST_Length(ST_ShortestLine(s.geom, r.geom))::numeric, 1),
       ST_ShortestLine(s.geom, r.geom)
FROM sites_pt s
CROSS JOIN LATERAL (SELECT name, geom FROM pune_roads
                    ORDER BY pune_roads.geom <-> s.geom LIMIT 1) r;

CREATE TABLE flood_buffer_pg (id serial PRIMARY KEY, river_name text, buffer_m int,
  geom geometry(MultiPolygon,32643));
INSERT INTO flood_buffer_pg (river_name, buffer_m, geom)
SELECT name, 200, ST_Multi(ST_Buffer(ST_Union(geom), 200))
FROM pune_rivers WHERE name IS NOT NULL
GROUP BY name ORDER BY SUM(ST_Length(geom)) DESC LIMIT 6;

-- check: expect 6 + 6 + 6 valid features
SELECT 'sites_pt', COUNT(*), bool_and(ST_IsValid(geom)) FROM sites_pt
UNION ALL SELECT 'access_ln', COUNT(*), bool_and(ST_IsValid(geom)) FROM access_ln
UNION ALL SELECT 'flood_buffer_pg', COUNT(*), bool_and(ST_IsValid(geom)) FROM flood_buffer_pg;

PostGIS spatial queries and analysis

5 marks

Brief: at least five spatial analyses, each with objective, SQL query, result, screenshot and interpretation.

Analysis 1 – Roads exposed to river flooding (buffer, intersection, length)

Objective: length of road network inside each 200 m river flood buffer.

SELECT f.river_name,
       ROUND(SUM(ST_Length(ST_Intersection(r.geom, f.geom)))::numeric / 1000, 2) AS road_km_exposed
FROM flood_buffer_pg f JOIN pune_roads r ON ST_Intersects(r.geom, f.geom)
GROUP BY f.river_name ORDER BY road_km_exposed DESC;

Output: road length (km) inside each river flood buffer, ranked from highest to lowest.

Interpretation: river corridors with the most exposed road length are where monsoon flooding most disrupts mobility and emergency access.

Analysis 2 – Surface water per ward (area, intersection)

Objective: ward area and the share covered by lakes and reservoirs.

SELECT w.ward_id, w.ward_name,
       ROUND((MAX(ST_Area(w.geom)) / 1e6)::numeric, 2) AS ward_km2,
       ROUND((100 * COALESCE(SUM(ST_Area(ST_Intersection(w.geom, b.geom))), 0)
              / MAX(ST_Area(w.geom)))::numeric, 2) AS water_pct
FROM pune_wards w LEFT JOIN pune_waterbodies b ON ST_Intersects(w.geom, b.geom)
GROUP BY w.ward_id, w.ward_name ORDER BY water_pct;

Output: area of each ward (km²) and the percentage covered by lakes and reservoirs.

Interpretation: wards with almost no surface water depend fully on piped supply and groundwater (indicator I2).

Analysis 3 – Road density per ward (length)

Objective: km of road per km² as a proxy for sealed surfaces.

SELECT w.ward_id, w.ward_name,
       ROUND(((COALESCE(SUM(ST_Length(ST_Intersection(w.geom, r.geom))), 0) / 1000)
              / (MAX(ST_Area(w.geom)) / 1e6))::numeric, 2) AS road_km_per_km2
FROM pune_wards w LEFT JOIN pune_roads r ON ST_Intersects(w.geom, r.geom)
GROUP BY w.ward_id, w.ward_name ORDER BY road_km_per_km2 DESC;

Output: road density for each ward in km of road per km².

Interpretation: dense road networks mark the urban core, where runoff is highest (indicator I3).

Analysis 4 – Nearest water body (nearest feature, distance)

Objective: nearest lake or reservoir to each ward and the distance to it.

SELECT w.ward_name, nb.name AS nearest_waterbody, ROUND(nb.dist_m::numeric) AS dist_m
FROM pune_wards w
CROSS JOIN LATERAL (
  SELECT b.name, ST_Distance(ST_PointOnSurface(w.geom), b.geom) AS dist_m
  FROM pune_waterbodies b
  ORDER BY b.geom <-> ST_PointOnSurface(w.geom) LIMIT 1) nb
ORDER BY dist_m DESC;

Output: the nearest lake or reservoir to each ward and the distance to it in metres.

Interpretation: the indexed K-nearest-neighbour operator finds wards far from any surface water (indicator I4).

Analysis 5 – Sites by ward and flood zone (spatial join, within, transformation)

Objective: which ward contains each monitoring site, whether it is in a flood zone, and its latitude/longitude.

SELECT s.site_name, w.ward_name,
       EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM flood_buffer_pg f WHERE ST_Within(s.geom, f.geom)) AS in_flood_zone,
       ROUND(ST_X(ST_Transform(s.geom, 4326))::numeric, 5) AS lon,
       ROUND(ST_Y(ST_Transform(s.geom, 4326))::numeric, 5) AS lat
FROM sites_pt s LEFT JOIN pune_wards w ON ST_Contains(w.geom, s.geom);

Output: each monitoring site with its ward, a flood-zone flag and latitude/longitude.

Interpretation: links sites to the responsible ward and flags those at flood risk; coordinates are converted for the web map.

Analysis 6 – WSERI composite index per ward

Objective: combine I1–I4 into the risk index and classify every ward. The full query is in the report and the SQL file.

-- after building ward_wseri (see report, Analysis 6):
ALTER TABLE ward_wseri ADD COLUMN risk_class text;
UPDATE ward_wseri SET risk_class = CASE
  WHEN wseri >= 0.8 THEN 'Very High' WHEN wseri >= 0.6 THEN 'High'
  WHEN wseri >= 0.4 THEN 'Moderate'  WHEN wseri >= 0.2 THEN 'Low'
  ELSE 'Very Low' END;

SELECT risk_class, COUNT(*) AS wards FROM ward_wseri
GROUP BY risk_class ORDER BY MIN(wseri) DESC;

Output: a WSERI score and risk class for every ward, plus the number of wards in each class.

Interpretation: High and Very High wards combine flood exposure, dense roads and little surface water – priorities for green infrastructure, lake restoration and resilient drainage.

Summary: Six spatial analyses were run in pgAdmin using buffer, intersection, area, length, nearest-neighbour, spatial join, within/contains and coordinate transformation. Analyses 1–5 each produce one indicator or supporting result, and Analysis 6 combines them into the ward-level WSERI risk index.

Thesis research

GeoAI Framework for Automated Rural Road Extraction in the Complex Rural Landscapes of Maval and Mahabaleshwar Talukas, Maharashtra

M.Sc. thesis · Remote sensing, GeoAI and deep learning

Rural roads are vital infrastructure for connectivity, access and regional development, yet they are hard to extract automatically: they are narrow, winding, often broken, and spectrally similar to vegetation, soil and terrain. My thesis builds a GeoAI workflow that extracts rural roads from Indian LISS-IV (5.8 m) imagery using an ensemble of DPMSRE-Net and TransUNet, tests how well it works in flat versus hilly landscapes, and maps how road networks changed across 2015, 2020 and 2025.

Maval Taluka

Pune District

Flatter, less complex rural landscape.

Mahabaleshwar Taluka

Satara District

Hilly, complex Western Ghats terrain.

Aim

To develop and evaluate an ensemble GeoAI framework for automated rural road extraction from LISS-IV imagery across the contrasting landscapes of Maval and Mahabaleshwar, and to assess how the extracted road networks changed between 2015 and 2025.

Objectives and research questions

Objective 1 · Automated rural road extraction. Develop and evaluate an ensemble of DPMSRE-Net and TransUNet for LISS-IV imagery and compare its performance in flat (Maval) and hilly (Mahabaleshwar) terrain.
RQ1: How accurately can the ensemble extract rural road networks across these contrasting landscapes?
Objective 2 · Temporal road-network change. Quantify spatial and temporal changes in the extracted rural road networks at five-year intervals: 2015, 2020 and 2025.
RQ2: How has the spatial configuration of rural roads changed from 2015 to 2025?

Models

DPMSRE-Net

Parallel CNN–Transformer hybrid

Dual encoder combining a CNN with a Swin Transformer, with MSAF and MSCA modules, trained on labelled road tiles.

TransUNet

Serial CNN–Transformer hybrid

CNN encoder feeding a Transformer (patch embedding and self-attention) and then a decoder, trained on the same tiles.

Ensemble

Final road map

Probability maps from both models combined by weighted averaging (soft voting) or stacking with an optional meta-learner, with weights tuned on the validation set.

Training setup: loss α·Dice + β·BCE (e.g. α = 0.7, β = 0.3), AdamW optimiser, learning rate 1e-4 with scheduler, batch size 4–16, 100–300 epochs with early stopping.
